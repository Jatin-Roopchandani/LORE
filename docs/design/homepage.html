How LORE answers a question

Five steps, the same for every book you add. Here's one question about A Study in Scarlet.

01 · YOU ASK

Ask it like you'd ask a friend who's read the book.

Vague, specific, about one chapter or the whole series. LORE splits it into the parts that need answering.

02 · IT SEARCHES THE GRAPH

It finds the people, places and links that matter.

Every book becomes a map of who knows whom, where things happen and what happens when. The answer starts there, not with a guess.

03 · IT READS THE PASSAGES

It goes back to the text.

The exact passages behind those links are pulled from the book, and every fact must quote one.

04 · IT CHECKS EVERY SENTENCE

A second pass cuts anything the book doesn't say.

Every fact is checked against its passage. What the book doesn't say is cut, and you never see it.

05 · IT WRITES THE ANSWER

Then it tells you, like a reader would.

The checked facts become a short answer and a few clear paragraphs, every sentence cited. Where it offers its own reading, it says so.

You asked How did Watson meet Sherlock Holmes?
Short answer

Through Stamford: an old dresser of Watson’s from Barts, met by chance at the Criterion Bar, who knew a man at the hospital laboratory looking for someone to share rooms. 13

Back in London and short of money, Watson was standing at the Criterion Bar when someone tapped him on the shoulder: young Stamford, who had been a dresser under him at Barts. 1 When Watson said he was looking for cheap lodgings, “That’s a strange thing,” Stamford remarked: he was the second man that day to say so. The first was a fellow at the chemical laboratory up at the hospital, who couldn’t find anyone to go halves with him on some rooms. 3

Stamford took him there. “Dr. Watson, Mr. Sherlock Holmes,” he said, introducing them, and Holmes, gripping his hand, told him at once that he had been in Afghanistan. 2
